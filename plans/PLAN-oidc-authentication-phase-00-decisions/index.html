    Phase 0: Research and decisions for OIDC authentication¶

    Context¶

    This is phase 0 of PLAN-oidc-authentication.md, and it is the first phase of that plan to be cut. It is a decisions phase: no production code changes. Its output is documentation — the audit and probe results recorded below, and committed answers written onto the master plan's open questions.

    The master plan cannot proceed without it. What phases 3 to 9 are depends on how open question 13 goes: whether a human session is a direct-bearer presentation of the IdP's own token, or an exchange of that token for an ordinary namespace key. And open question 14 — whether one credential may name more than one namespace — decides whether phase 4 is a mechanical rename with a handful of hand-written exceptions or a redesign of every authorisation check in the codebase.

    Phases 1 and 2 of the master plan were superseded: they were built as PLAN-auth-federation.md phase 3 and their Merged cells already name the merges that landed them. Those three merges were re-verified as merge commits and as ancestors of develop while planning this phase, so there is no previous phase of this plan to close out and the planning commit carries no close-out.

    Planning effort: high. The master plan's Agent guidance reserves high effort for phases turning on design decisions and subtle correctness questions, and this phase is nothing but those.

    Scope¶

    In scope. Settling five of the master plan's open questions and recording the evidence that settled them:

    • Open question 13 — direct-bearer versus exchange-based human sessions. The central question.
    • Open question 14 — multi-namespace authorisation, including where the line sits between a multi-namespace credential and the namespace trust mechanism that already exists.
    • Open question 5 — whether caller_is_admin can drop its system-namespace half now that a cluster-admin scope exists. The master plan says this should be taken alongside 13 and it is, here.
    • Open question 7 — nonce versus bounded-delay revocation. This is no longer an independent decision; it is a consequence of 13 and is recorded as one.
    • Open question 11 — whether namespace keys are renamed to service-account credentials. Cheap, and it decides whether phase 6 exists at all, so it is answered before phase 6 is cut rather than at the time.

    Plus the housekeeping that the survey below showed this plan needs: correcting the master plan's stale file references and its dead CLAUDE.md pointer, and filing the deferred work the survey turned up.

    Out of scope.

    • Any production code change. Nothing under shakenfist/ is committed by this phase. The feasibility probe of step 0b is deliberately thrown away; see D4.
    • Open questions 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6, which are already answered or subsumed on the master plan.
    • Open question 8 (inter-node authentication), which the master plan argues should be left open on purpose, and this phase does not overturn that.
    • Open questions 9, 10 and 12 — CLI flow choice, client-side credential cache, documentation surface. The first two are client-python's and are blocked on phase 3's discovery work; the third is phase 8's own planning problem once 13 and 14 are settled.
    • Deciding the content of phases 3 to 9. Phase 0 may delete or rewrite an Execution row that its answers make wrong, and must say so in its commit message, but it does not plan those phases.
    • Fixing the two defects the survey found. They are filed as issues by step 0f and left for their own work; see D5.

    What the survey found¶

    Surveyed at 32de6cec7f. Every substantive claim the master plan makes about the tree still holds. Nothing in its premises has been invalidated by the three months since PLAN-auth-federation-phase-05-oidc-plan-refresh.md brought it current, and the two numbers its argument leans on hardest are exactly right: request_namespace() has 57 call sites outside the tests and shakenfist/external_api/artifact.py holds 17 of them.

    # 58 lines; one of them is the definition itself. The .tox exclusion
# matters -- without it a plain grep(1) also finds the two installed
# copies of the package under .tox/ and returns 172.
grep -rn 'request_namespace()' --include='*.py' . |
    grep -v '/tests/' | grep -v '/\.tox/'

    Nine things are nevertheless worth recording, and the last is the important one.

    2. CLAUDE.md no longer exists¶

    The master plan's Prompt section says to consult CLAUDE.md for build commands and conventions. It was folded into AGENTS.md and docs/ by 2f443906ff. Corrected in the planning commit.

    3. The missing-scopes wildcard default has three¶

    enforcement sites, not two

    The master plan names two places where api_scopes.satisfies() treats a None scope list as a wildcard: caller_is_admin and _enforce_scope. There is a third, added since the refresh, and it runs in the opposite direction:

    Site What it does
    base.py:292 caller_is_admin — grants administrative access
    base.py:2464 _enforce_scope — grants the derived scope
    auth.py:1109-1118 caps what scopes a mapping rule may grant

    The third is a narrowing check: a caller holding None may author a rule granting anything, because None means "legacy operator, unrestricted". An issuer-dependent missing-claim default has to answer this site too, and the answer is not simply "deny" — denying there would stop an IdP-authenticated administrator authoring any rule at all. Step 0b's brief names this explicitly, because it is the kind of site a probe that only checks "does the request authenticate" will miss.

    4. A foreign token cannot reach verify_token's body¶

    verify_token (base.py:1422) opens with verify_jwt_in_request(...) at base.py:1425 — flask_jwt_extended, validating against the cluster's own secret. An IdP-issued token fails there, before any Shaken Fist code runs. And everything downstream of it — parse_jwt_identity(), request_namespace(), caller_scopes(), _enforce_scope — reads flask_jwt_extended's request context through get_jwt() and get_jwt_identity().

    So the master plan's claim that discriminating between token shapes is "a change to one function rather than to a decorator stack" is true, but narrower than it sounds: the change is a split ahead of the flask_jwt_extended call, and whether direct-bearer can live "inside the single existing enforcement path" reduces to one concrete question — can an externally-validated token populate that request context, or must every downstream reader grow a second path? That is the whole of what step 0b has to find out, which is why the probe is cheap and tightly scoped (D4).

    5. Discovery is genuinely absent¶

    Confirmed: nothing outside docs/ fetches .well-known/openid-configuration, and the only .well-known strings in the tree are JWKS URLs in tests and documentation examples. Phase 3 remains entirely unbuilt, as the master plan says.

    6. The _service_key nonce bypass is still live, and now bounded¶

    base.py:1449-1456 skips the nonce check entirely for a token whose key name is exactly _service_key, with a comment deferring its retirement to PLAN-auth-federation-phase-02-key-objects.md — a phase which is Complete. It is bounded rather than open: _validate_key_name (auth.py:117-133) and credentials.is_reserved_key_name() now reject that name on both the namespace-create and key-create paths, so no new key can occupy it and only a token minted years ago could exercise the bypass. Retirable, and out of scope here. Filed by step 0f.

    7. Issue #3672 is still live and is a phase 6 prerequisite¶

    AuthNamespaceKeysEndpoint.get (auth.py:591-595) returns a list of key names, read from Namespace.keys's legacy nonced_keys compatibility view. NamespaceKey.external_view() (namespace_key.py:404-418) exists and already exposes expiry, scopes and provenance — nothing serves it. So a key's expiry, scopes and provenance remain unreadable through any API, which matters here because phase 6's whole subject is presenting keys to operators as service-account credentials. Recorded on the master plan as a phase 6 prerequisite by step 0d.

    8. "Claim" now means two things in this codebase¶

    docs/glossary.md:61-67 gained capacity claim — a NamespaceClaim (namespace_claim.py:95) reserving cluster capacity for a namespace — and explicitly warns against confusing it with a mapping rule's bound claims. The glossary has done the disambiguation work; what remains is discipline. Every answer this phase writes says "bound claims" or "identity claims" and never a bare "claims".

    Relatedly, app.py:456-466 advertises trusted-issuers, mapping-rules and federated-exchange as client-visible capabilities. Whatever human-login surface the master plan settles on will need one too — a constraint on later phases, recorded so nobody rediscovers it.

    But the shape the question was trying to detect is already in production, for a machine. The CI conductor is one actor holding a separate credential in every namespace it works in, all of them named conductor, because a namespace key can name exactly one namespace — fourteen of them in one sample and eight an hour later, so it is the invariant and not the count that carries the argument. That is not a hypothetical boundary. D7 makes drawing it a required part of answering question 14, and it means the audit in step 0a has a worked example to check its conclusions against rather than only the code.

    One caveat on reading this as support for exchange: a machine that mints a credential per namespace at provisioning time is not the same problem as a person who must hold one per namespace in a credential cache and choose between them per request. The conductor's keys are created by automation that already knows which namespace it is working in. A human's would not be.

    Decisions¶

    D1 — This phase ships no production code. The deliverable is the master plan's committed answers plus this plan's appendices. This follows the repository's established shape for a decisions phase; PLAN-api-input-validation-phase-00-decisions.md is the worked precedent, including the convention that measurements live in the phase plan and decisions live on the master plan.

    D2 — Open question 13's first piece of evidence is unobtainable, and is replaced rather than faked. The survey measured it and found a population of one human in one namespace, in the only deployment that exists. The honest reading is that sfcbr cannot discriminate between the two options, not that it votes for exchange.

    A reviewer could reasonably object that this is the wrong population: Shaken Fist's intended deployment is a corporate cloud with staff, and the absence of humans from a CI cluster is an artefact of what sfcbr is for. That objection is correct, and it is exactly why the measurement is recorded as a removed piece of evidence rather than as an answer. What it rules out is the master plan's hope that a survey would settle the question cheaply. What it does not do is license guessing at a distribution we have never observed.

    D3 — The decision therefore rests on the two things that are obtainable: the call-site audit and the feasibility probe. The audit (0a) tells us what multi-namespace authorisation would cost, and the probe (0b) tells us whether direct-bearer can be expressed inside the single enforcement path. Both are architectural facts about our own code rather than facts about other people's deployments, and both are decisive in the sense that a bad answer to either disqualifies an option. If the probe shows direct-bearer cannot live inside the one path, "authorisation lives in one place" — which the master plan's Mission says it will not trade away — settles question 13 without needing the distribution at all.

    D4 — The probe is narrowly scoped and is not committed. Survey finding 4 reduced it to one question: can an externally-validated token populate flask_jwt_extended's request context such that parse_jwt_identity(), caller_scopes() and _enforce_scope read it unchanged?     Summary: mechanical, with a concentrated redesign in one corner¶

    It is a mechanical change, and the 57 sites do come out lopsided — but the lopsidedness is not where open question 14 expected it, and the 16 sites in bucket (C) are not exceptions that can be hand-written one at a time. They are one design decision applied sixteen times.

    A and B together are 39 of the 55 real sites (71%), and they are the same edit.     So the authorisation tests really are a rename plus three helper signatures. The master plan's framing — "a change to the shape of every authorisation check in the codebase" — overstates it, because the checks are not 39 independent shapes.

    Bucket C is where the work is, and it is a design decision, not a sweep. At these 16 sites request_namespace() is not asking a question; it is answering one — "which namespace does this object go in", "whose label is this", "which namespace do I mint a proxy token for". A credential naming several namespaces has no answer, so every one of these sites needs the namespace to arrive from somewhere else. They decompose into exactly three decisions:

    1. Eight sites default a target namespace when the request did not name one (if not namespace: namespace = request_namespace(), and the else: arms of the three delete-all routes, plus base.py:330). One decision: either a multi-namespace caller must name its target namespace explicitly on these routes, or a credential carries a designated default. This is open question 14's own sub-question "does a request name its target namespace explicitly" — and these eight sites are its entire blast radius.
    2. Four sites build a URL out of the caller's namespace (label.py:45, instance.py:903, instance.py:1021 and artifact.py:624). These follow decision 1: once the target namespace is known, the URL is built from it.
    3. Four sites mint a node-to-node proxy token (base.py:1765, base.py:1994, external_api/blob.py:62, artifact.py:605), all via get_api_token(..., namespace=request_namespace()), which creates a transient _service_key_* in a single namespace (namespace.py:494-519). This is the one thing in the audit that genuinely does not have a mechanical answer, and the plan does not mention it: a multi-namespace request that crosses a proxy hop is re-authenticated as a single namespace on the far side, so it silently loses entitlements mid-request. Either token minting grows multi-namespace support, or the proxy must resolve the request to one namespace before forwarding. See "Not anticipated" below.

    Verdict. Phase 4 is a mechanical change — three helper signatures, one repeated idiom, and three request-shape decisions applied to sixteen value sites — not a redesign of the authorisation model. The redesign risk is concentrated in one place the plan had not identified: the four proxy-token sites, where the single-namespace credential is re-minted rather than re-used.

    Step 0b — Feasibility probe: can direct-bearer live inside the single enforcement path?¶

    Probed at 32de6cec7f in a discarded worktree. Nothing committed; the diff below is the whole of what was changed and is thrown away. Every claim here is from a passing test in that worktree, not from reading.

    Answer in one line. Yes, the request context can be populated so that parse_jwt_identity(), request_namespace(), caller_scopes(), _enforce_scope and caller_is_admin read a foreign token with no change to any of them — but only because the probe forges the two claims Shaken Fist's own minting creates, and the two things direct-bearer was supposed to buy do not survive that forgery.

    1. The mechanism, and whether it is private API¶

    Three mechanisms were built and measured.

    What it is Private API? Verdict
    A Validate with federation.validate_token(), then assign flask.g._jwt_extended_jwt, _jwt_extended_jwt_header, _jwt_extended_jwt_user, _jwt_extended_jwt_location Yes — four undocumented attribute names, copied from flask_jwt_extended/view_decorators.py:113-116 Works.     B @jwt.decode_key_loader, returning the issuer's JWKS key for a foreign iss No — a documented JWTManager callback Authenticates, and flask_jwt_extended populates its own context. But it silently drops three checks (below)

    There is no public "set the verified token for this request" API in flask_jwt_extended 4.7.4 (confirmed by reading utils.py and view_decorators.py in the installed package). So the honest answer is: the mechanism needs private API, or an undocumented aliasing guarantee. Mechanism A is the one worth costing, because it is the only one that keeps federation.validate_token() in the path.

    What mechanism B costs, measured rather than argued:

    • JWT_DECODE_ALGORITHMS is global, so it must be widened to ['HS256'] + federation.ALLOWED_ALGORITHMS for the whole application. The classic consequence — forge an HS256 token using the issuer's public key as the HMAC secret — does not land, but only because PyJWT refuses an asymmetric key as an HMAC secret (jwt/algorithms.py:357), at both encode and decode. The forgery had to be assembled by hand to test the decode side at all.
    • JWT_DECODE_AUDIENCE and JWT_DECODE_ISSUER are global, so the per-issuer audience binding is lost: a token minted for somebody-else verified happily through verify_jwt_in_request() while federation.validate_token() refused it.
    • flask_jwt_extended's _decode_jwt() passes no require list, so a token with no exp verified. validate_token() requires exp, iss and aud.

    2. What the context has to be forged with¶

    An IdP sub is the IdP's subject. parse_jwt_identity() (shakenfist/util/access_tokens.py:74-80) splits on : and demands exactly two parts, so alice@example.com raises CannotParseJWTIdentity and GitHub's repo:x:ref:y raises it too. Populating the context with the IdP's claims untouched therefore authenticates and then dies: under mechanism B a good foreign token answered 500, because CannotParseJWTIdentity is neither caught by verify_token's except (TypeError, ValueError) nor by handle_authorization_exceptions (base.py:2304).

    So the probe derives a namespace by evaluating mapping rules against the token's claims, per request, and writes sub as '<namespace>:<key_name_prefix>'. With that one forgery everything downstream works unchanged — asserted directly inside a patched endpoint handler: get_jwt_identity() == 'staff:alice', parse_jwt_identity() == ['staff', 'alice'], request_namespace() == 'staff', caller_scopes() is None, and the IdP's own claims (email, iss, groups) all still present. The cost is in the same assertion: sub no longer holds alice@example.com. The IdP's identity is destroyed to make the identity readable.

    Two further consequences fell out and are measured, not inferred:

    • The audit trail records a key that does not exist. log_token_use → _token_use_event recorded ('staff', 'alice', 'token used to authenticate request'). There is no key named alice; the IdP identity appears in no event.
    • The context can name exactly one namespace. With the same identity matching rules in two namespaces, request_namespace() still returns one string. Multi-namespace — the one consideration open question 13 says favours direct-bearer — is not delivered by fitting inside this path. Delivering it means changing request_namespace()'s contract, which is step 0a's 57 sites.

    If it could not be done, the second path would be four functions, not 57 call sites: base.py:291 (caller_is_admin), base.py:310 (caller_scopes), base.py:2462 (_enforce_scope) and access_tokens.py:76 (parse_jwt_identity) are the only non-test readers of get_jwt() / get_jwt_identity(). A fifth, incidental one is the request-log identity lookup in log_request (base.py:2104-2112), which calls decode_token() inside a bare except Exception: pass, so a foreign token raises there and the request is logged with no identity. (That site reads a claim named identity, which this cluster does not mint — the identity claim key is sub — so it already yields None for cluster tokens too. Pre-existing, out of this step's scope, not filed.) The number that matters is small, and it is worth saying plainly: "every downstream authorisation reader" is four functions.

    An error-translation layer is also required either way. handle_authorization_exceptions catches only flask_jwt_extended and PyJWT exception classes, so federation.TokenValidationFailed escaped as 500 until the probe mapped it onto NoAuthorizationError. Three tests failed exactly that way before the mapping was added.

    4. The consequence at site 3, which is not "safer"¶

    An issuer-dependent default of "deny for tokens we did not mint" has two possible implementations and both go wrong at site 3, in opposite directions.

    Normalise the claim to [] at the boundary. Then site 3 is never reached: the request is refused one layer earlier by _enforce_scope for rule.write. An IdP-authenticated administrator cannot author any mapping rule at all — not a wildcard one, not a narrow one, not even one granting [] — and the refusal reads token is not scoped for this operation, which says nothing about the issuer-dependent default that caused it. OIDC login would be unable to administer federation, which is close to the main thing an operator logs in to do, and it would present as an inexplicable 403.

    Change satisfies() instead, leaving the claim absent. Then site 3 goes the other way and fails open. caller_scopes() (base.py:302-312) tests held is None itself, before it ever consults satisfies(), and site 3 reads caller_scopes(). So the cap would be lifted — the IdP-authenticated caller may author a rule granting *, which is a standing instruction to mint wildcard keys in system, exactly the escalation the comment at auth.py:1093-1105 exists to prevent — while sites 1 and 2 denied. One change, fail-closed at two sites and fail-open at the third.

    The reason is that None is read in two directions. At sites 1 and 2 it is a grant ("holds everything"), where deny is the safe default. At site 3 it is a cap ("restricted by nothing"), where deny means "holds nothing" and the safe-looking default refuses every rule. A single issuer-dependent default cannot satisfy both.

    The only default that makes all three sites coherent is to give the foreign session a real scope set, synthesised from the mapping rule that authenticated it. Measured: with that, site 2 enforces per verb and site 3 caps at what the login granted. But two things follow. The cap is then only as strong as the login rule — a rule granting ['*'] reproduces the uncapped case exactly (measured). And computing it is the exchange's own computation, run per request, against no stored key: direct-bearer collapses into exchange without persistence, minus the nonce and minus an audit row that names a real credential.

    5. Verdict¶

    Direct-bearer can live inside the single enforcement path, in the narrow sense that no downstream reader needs to change. It needs private flask_jwt_extended API (four g attributes) plus an exception-translation layer, and it fits only by forging the claims Shaken Fist's own minting produces. The fit destroys the IdP's identity, attributes every audit event to a key that does not exist, delivers exactly one namespace per request, and leaves a missing-scopes default that cannot be made issuer-dependent coherently across the three sites without synthesising scopes from a mapping rule — at which point what has been built is exchange, enforced identically, with revocation and auditability given up. On this evidence "authorisation lives in one place" is preserved by making direct-bearer indistinguishable from exchange at the point of enforcement.

    Per the master plan, the missing-scopes wildcard is not a bug; nothing was filed and nothing was fixed.

    4. What the docs tell an operator about human credentials¶

    • docs/user_guide/authentication.md is the only place that addresses humans. It says: "Normally your administrator will create a key per user, but it's also possible to create a key per system -- there are no real rules imposed". It then tells the user to put a .shakenfist file in their home directory with namespace, key and apiurl. It also says keys may carry expiry and scopes, and that those cannot be read back through any API (the #3672 gap).
    • docs/operator_guide/authentication.md says nothing about human credentials as such. Its "Federated identity" section describes trading an external identity for a namespace key, and its worked example is GitHub Actions; it names "a service account in an Authentik instance" as the other case. Both are workloads. Its human-adjacent content is the generated-key format (sfk_...), key expiry and reaping, and the note that an administrator must add keys by hand (sf-client namespace add-key, or POST /auth/namespaces/<ns>/keys).

    So the guidance is: an administrator hand-creates a long-lived key per person in that person's one namespace, and the person keeps it in a file. No document describes a person belonging to several namespaces, nor any login flow for people. The user guide's model is one namespace per user ("the administrator ... will have created a namespace to contain the resources you create").

    5. Where open question 13's distribution evidence would have to come from¶

    It would have to come from real deployments with staff: how many namespaces each person at a handful of operators' clusters is actually in. We do not have it. sfcbr is a CI cluster with no staff, so it holds one human-held key (roberto) in one namespace, which is a sample of size one and says nothing about a distribution. This repository's own tooling provisions no person anywhere, so it cannot stand in for that evidence either. The conductor's many credentials show the multi-namespace shape for a machine, but a machine whose provisioning automation knows each namespace is not a person choosing between credentials. We have not surveyed any other Shaken Fist deployment, and I have no data that would let me say whether the usual answer is one namespace or several.
